# *PLINTH-LABS — CAN THIS BUSINESS MAKE MONEY? (1 OF 1)

**Prepared:** September 23, 2026
**Subject:** Commercial assessment of *Plinth-Labs, a Sag Harbor nonprofit proposing prefabricated workforce ADUs
**Companion to:** `research/east-end-adu/market-research.md` (September 18, 2026)
**Sources:** Company website, The East Hampton Star, 27East / Sag Harbor Express, Sag Harbor Village records, and the regulatory and cost research in the companion memo

---

## 1. THE ANSWER UP FRONT

**No, not as currently structured. The revenue model is arithmetically incapable of doing what it claims, and the cost basis is understated by roughly 2x. But the underlying business is viable if it is restructured from a self-funding revolving fund into a fee-for-service design-build practice that plugs into the $100.74 million of public money already flowing through the East End.**

Four findings drive that conclusion.

- **The self-funding flywheel cannot compound.** At a 10% royalty on capped rent, one unit in service returns roughly $3,107 per year. Financing one new $232,000 unit per year requires approximately 75 units already in service. The Town of East Hampton caps accessory dwelling permits at 200 town-wide. **If Plinth-Labs built every legally permitted ADU in East Hampton, the royalty stream would fund 2.7 new units per year.** The mechanism that the entire pitch rests on does not work at any achievable scale.
- **The cost basis is off by about 2x.** The $128,000 figure cited to the Village Board is a 360 sq ft module built as an architecture thesis in Germany. Landed in Suffolk County with foundation, crane, utility connection, an Article 6 sanitary system, and soft costs, the same unit is $178,000 in the best case and $232,000 in the base case. Every downstream number in the pitch inherits the error.
- **The employer underwriting ask is uneconomic and will not scale.** Asking a Sag Harbor restaurant to fund $232,000 of capex for one bed costs roughly $29,500 per year amortized. A $1,000 per month housing stipend costs $12,000 per year and carries no capital lock-up. Employers will fund sponsorships at $10,000 to $30,000. They will not fund units.
- **Plinth-Labs is competing against free money instead of channeling it.** Southampton offers homeowners $125,000 at zero interest with no royalty and no equity give-up. East Hampton offers $100,000 deferred. New York State Plus One offers up to $125,000. A homeowner who can access those keeps 100% of the rent and owes nothing to anyone. Plinth's offer is worse on every axis except one, and that one exception is the whole business.

**The one exception is the real company.** Plinth's offer requires zero cash from the homeowner. For an asset-rich, cash-poor, credit-averse long-time local family, that is genuinely differentiated. That segment exists on the East End in numbers. But serving it is a financing and delivery service, not a revolving fund.

## 3. WHERE THE COST BASIS BREAKS

The €110,000 thesis unit is a real accomplishment and a useless benchmark. A German student project carries no Suffolk County sanitary approval, no New York State Department of State modular insignia under 19 NYCRR Part 1209, no Hamptons crane and trucking cost, no BHPAR review, and very likely subsidized labor and materials.

### 3.1 All-in cost, 360 sq ft detached unit, Sag Harbor

| Line | Optimistic | Base | Downside |
|---|---|---|---|
| Module, delivered | $128,160 | $162,000 | $198,000 |
| Implied $/sq ft, module only | $356 | $450 | $550 |
| Foundation, crane, utility connection, site work | $35,000 | $45,000 | $70,000 |
| Article 6 sanitary (I/A OWTS) gross | $30,000 | $30,000 | $40,000 |
| Less Suffolk County and NYS septic grants | ($30,000) | ($25,000) | ($15,000) |
| Soft costs: survey, permits, design review, engineering | $15,000 | $20,000 | $30,000 |
| **All-in project cost** | **$178,160** | **$232,000** | **$323,000** |
| **All-in $/sq ft** | **$495** | **$644** | **$897** |

*Module pricing benchmarked to Ecocor at $325 to $450/sq ft turnkey, BrightBuilt Home at $450 to $600/sq ft including sitework, and Abodu at $537/sq ft in California before a New York labor premium. Septic grants are competitive and not assured. Property tax uplift on the improvement is not modeled.*

**The pitch number is the first line of a six-line build.** Even the optimistic column is 39% above the figure presented to the Village Board. The base case is 81% above it.

Note also that small units carry the worst cost-per-square-foot in the entire product range. A 360 sq ft unit needs a full kitchen, a full bathroom, a heat pump, an electrical service, a foundation and a sanitary system, and it spreads all of that over 360 sq ft rather than 600. **Sag Harbor Village's 280 sq ft minimum and 600 sq ft maximum means the product should be built at 600 sq ft, not 360.** The marginal 240 sq ft costs perhaps $200 per sq ft in envelope and finish while the rent ceiling rises from the studio cap of $2,589 to the one-bedroom cap of $3,092. That single design decision is worth more to the model than any manufacturing efficiency available to them.

## 4. WHERE THE REVENUE MODEL BREAKS

### 4.1 The homeowner deal

Rent is capped. East Hampton's 2025 to 2026 schedule sets 130% of HUD Fair Market Rent at $2,589 per month for a studio and $3,092 for a one-bedroom. There is no upside case above the cap, ever.

| Line | 360 sq ft studio |
|---|---|
| Gross annual rent at cap | $31,068 |
| Less opex: tax uplift, insurance, maintenance, vacancy at 30% | ($9,320) |
| **Net operating income** | **$21,748** |
| Less Plinth royalty at 10% of gross rent | ($3,107) |
| **Homeowner net after royalty** | **$18,641** |

**Payback to the homeowner, years to repay construction cost:**

| Cost case | No royalty | With 10% royalty |
|---|---|---|
| Optimistic, $178,160 | 8.2 | 9.6 |
| Base, $232,000 | 10.7 | 12.4 |
| Downside, $323,000 | 14.9 | 17.3 |

So the homeowner's proposition is: surrender the backyard, accept landlord obligations and an income-and-employment-restricted tenant, collect no net cash for twelve years, and then pay a royalty in perpetuity on an asset you have already bought outright.

### 4.2 Against the public alternative

| | Plinth-Labs | Southampton CHF loan |
|---|---|---|
| Homeowner cash at close | $0 | $107,000 (base case, $232,000 less $125,000 loan) |
| Interest rate | n/a | 0%, 20-year term, repayment begins year 2 |
| Years to positive cash flow | 12.4 | Immediate |
| Annual cash flow after debt service | $0 until year 12.4, then $18,641 | $15,498 from year 2 |
| Cash-on-cash yield | Undefined (no cash in) | 14.5% |
| Perpetual royalty | Yes | None |
| Owns asset free and clear | Year 12.4, encumbered by royalty | Year 20, unencumbered |

For any homeowner who can raise $107,000, the town program wins outright. **Plinth's addressable customer is therefore not "homeowners." It is homeowners who cannot or will not put up cash and cannot or will not borrow.** That is a legitimate and underserved segment, and it is also the segment with the least capacity to absorb a cost overrun, the least sophistication about a perpetual royalty on their title, and the most to lose if the venture fails mid-construction.

### 4.3 The employer underwrite

| Option for a Sag Harbor employer | Annual cost | Flexibility | Capital locked |
|---|---|---|---|
| Underwrite one ADU at $232,000, amortized 10 yrs at 5% | ~$29,500 | None. Tied to one property, one unit | $232,000 |
| Housing stipend at $1,000/mo per employee | $12,000 | Full. Scales up and down seasonally | $0 |
| Seasonal wage premium | Variable | Full | $0 |
| Sponsorship gift of $25,000 to a 501(c)(3) | $25,000 once, ~$15,000 after tax | n/a | $25,000 |
| **Master lease, 5 yrs at studio cap** | **$31,068** | Low, but delivers a controlled unit | **$0** |

Full capex underwriting costs an employer roughly 2.5x a stipend for a single bed with none of the flexibility. Restaurant margins on the East End are thin and violently seasonal. **This will not happen at scale, and the pitch depends on it happening at scale.**

The master lease is the ask they should be making instead. It costs the employer no capital, converts capex to opex, guarantees the homeowner's rent for five years, makes the unit financeable by a bank or a CDFI, and solves the employer's actual problem, which is a bed in July, not a balance sheet asset.

### 4.4 The flywheel

This is the decisive arithmetic.

| Royalty rate on gross rent | Annual royalty per unit | Units in service needed to fund one new unit per year | Units needed to fund 10 per year |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | $1,553 | 149 | 1,494 |
| 10% | $3,107 | 75 | 747 |
| 25% | $7,767 | 30 | 299 |
| 50% | $15,534 | 15 | 149 |

*Against a base-case unit cost of $232,000.*

East Hampton's entire statutory ceiling is 200 ADU permits town-wide and 40 per school district. Sag Harbor Village's current 20,000 sq ft and 70 ft width thresholds block six of every eight applications, per Fire Marshal Bruce Schiavoni. Southampton's 20,000 sq ft small-lot relief applies only in census-designated places under 500 persons per square mile, which excludes Hampton Bays, East Quogue, Speonk, Quiogue, Riverside and Shinnecock Hills.

**At a 10% royalty and a build of every permitted unit in East Hampton, the fund throws off 2.7 units per year. A 25% royalty gets to 6.7, and no homeowner accepts a quarter of gross rent in perpetuity on an asset they paid for.** The revolving fund is not a slow flywheel. It is not a flywheel.

## 5. WHAT THEY GOT RIGHT

Dismissing this would be a mistake. Several of the hardest things to acquire, they already have.

- **The market read is correct and the money is real.** The East End Community Housing Fund has collected $100.74 million through July 2026 on a voter-approved 0.5% transfer tax, with Southampton at $58 million and East Hampton at $32.18 million. Median year-round rent in East Hampton is $3,800, up 64% since 2019. The East End population grew 35% since 2010 while affordable stock grew 4%.
- **Political access is genuine and is an asset with real value.** A mayor calling the presentation excellent, a trustee asking "how can we help," and an unsolicited offer to help find a demonstration site is access that established developers spend years and retainers trying to buy. It is also perishable.
- **Prefab is the right delivery method for this specific market.** Hamptons custom construction runs $800 to $2,000 per sq ft with trades at $100 to $500 per hour. A module built in Maine or Pennsylvania carries that state's labor cost and ships into Suffolk County under a New York DOS insignia covering structural, mechanical, electrical and plumbing. **The wider the local labor premium, the larger the modular arbitrage, and the East End's premium is among the largest in the country.**
- **Nonprofit structure unlocks real advantages** if the 501(c)(3) is actually in place: employer contributions become deductible, foundation and CDFI capital opens up, and the organization becomes a partner to the towns' housing offices rather than a vendor competing with them.
- **Employer demand is real.** Montauk's starting teacher salary is $65,000 against a cheapest listing of $1.3 million. The Long Island Association names housing cost and commute length as the binding constraint on staffing education, healthcare, tourism and agriculture.
- **Sag Harbor's 280 sq ft minimum, adopted November 2023, suits a small-module product,** and ADUs are as-of-right on qualifying village properties requiring only Building Department approval.

## 6. WHAT THE RAUS EXPERIENCE DOES NOT TRANSFER

The founder's formative experience is RAUS, a Berlin hospitality-tech startup that has raised approximately $12 million and operates 50+ off-grid designer cabins across nine German states, with expansion into Austria. It is an excellent company. Almost none of its economics carry over.

| Variable | RAUS | East End ADU |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | Nightly rates, unregulated, seasonal peak pricing | Monthly rent hard-capped at 130% HUD FMR |
| Tenant | Discretionary leisure traveler | Income- and employment-restricted essential worker |
| Occupancy | Company controls booking platform | Owner-occupancy rules, one-year minimum leases, tenant residency tests |
| Land | Farmland, leased from farmers | Someone else's residential backyard, encumbered by title |
| Sanitary | Composting toilets, fresh water tanks | Suffolk County Article 6, I/A OWTS at 19 mg/L nitrogen, SCDHS Form WWM-105, bedroom counting per Memorandum #19 |
| Code | Temporary or recreational structures | Permanent dwelling, NYS Uniform Code, DOS insignia, all-electric mandate |
| Design review | None material | BHPAR in Sag Harbor Village, plus the SANS overlay district |
| Structure | For-profit, venture funded | Nonprofit, no equity to sell |

**The off-grid, technologically independent design language is the part that transferred, and it is the part that matters least.** As the companion memo sets out, nothing in Article 6, the town permit caps, the lot size minimums, the rent caps or the architectural review is affected by how the dwelling makes electricity. Composting toilets get a cabin on Brandenburg farmland. They do not get a Suffolk County certificate of occupancy. If the venture wants a technical thesis that unlocks supply, it is decentralized wastewater, not decentralized energy.

## 7. THE VERSION THAT MAKES MONEY

Keep the mission, the brand, the political access and the product. Replace the financial engine.

### 7.1 Structural changes

1. **Stop competing with the $100.74 million. Become its delivery mechanism.** Position Plinth-Labs as the preferred design-build vendor to the Southampton CHF program, the East Hampton ADU CAP pilot and NYS Plus One. The homeowner brings subsidized capital. Plinth delivers the unit and the permit. This converts the towns from an audience into a distribution channel.
2. **Charge a contract margin, not a perpetual royalty.** A royalty on someone's home is hard to paper, hard to enforce, unattractive at closing, and as shown above raises trivial money. A 20% gross margin on a design-build contract raises roughly 15x more per unit, immediately, with no title encumbrance.
3. **Change the employer ask from capex to a master lease.** Five to ten years at the capped rent. No capital from the employer, guaranteed income for the homeowner, a financeable unit, and a solved staffing problem. Keep a sponsorship tier at $10,000 to $30,000 for the charitable-gift constituency.
4. **Build at 600 sq ft, not 360.** Same foundation, same sanitary system, same permit, materially better rent ceiling and materially better cost per square foot.
5. **Contract-manufacture. Never build a factory.** Katerra raised $2.4 billion and went bankrupt. Veev raised $600 million, hit a $1 billion valuation and liquidated for single-digit millions. Entekra, Blu Homes and Revolution Precrafted all died of the same disease: fixed factory overhead against lumpy, permit-gated demand. Qualify two DOS-insignia'd Northeast manufacturers (KBS Builders in South Paris ME, Westchester Modular in Wingdale NY, Simplex Homes in Scranton PA) and hold the design manufacturer-agnostic.
6. **Consider a hybrid structure:** a 501(c)(3) that holds the mission, the grant capital, the employer gifts and the tenant-matching function, alongside a for-profit design-build LLC that takes the contract margin. This preserves the deduction and the grant access while creating something the founders can actually own. It requires careful counsel on private benefit and self-dealing.
7. **Sell the service, not the box.** What the customer cannot get elsewhere is a pre-reviewed design, an assembled subsidy stack, a handled SCDHS filing, a vetted tenant and a delivery date. That bundle, not the architecture, is the product.

**Breakeven is roughly seven to eight units per year.** That is achievable on the East End by year three. Fifteen units supports a genuine small business. Getting to forty requires leaving the East End, and the obvious second market is New York City, which runs the same state code, the same modular insignia path and the same Plus One architecture, and which reopened Plus One financing on March 18, 2026 alongside a library of eleven DOB-reviewed pre-approved ADU designs.

**Capital required:** approximately $600,000 to $900,000 to cover eighteen months of overhead plus factory deposits before the first revenue lands. As a nonprofit with this political profile and this local philanthropic base, that is raisable. It also means the first two years are a fundraising business, not a construction business, and the founders should be honest with themselves about that.

## 8. RISKS AND MITIGANTS

- **Risk: founder capability gap.** Two 2025 graduates in architecture and biomedical engineering, with no construction, development, land-use or capital markets experience, attempting a business whose binding constraints are all construction, land use and capital.
  **Mitigant:** hire or partner with a licensed New York general contractor, a New York professional engineer for the sanitary work, and a Suffolk County land-use attorney before the first contract is signed. Recruit a board with a local developer and a housing finance practitioner. The design talent is the part they already have.
- **Risk: the demonstration unit consumes all the capital and all the credibility.** First projects run long and over budget, and a highly visible failure in a two-square-mile village is terminal for the brand.
  **Mitigant:** do the first unit on a cooperative, forgiving site with a fully pre-screened sanitary path, price it as a loss leader funded by grant capital, and do not sign a second contract until the first has a certificate of occupancy.
- **Risk: perpetual royalty is unenforceable or unmarketable, and may cloud title.**
  **Mitigant:** replace it with contract margin per Section 7.1. If any ongoing participation is retained, structure it as an optional property management fee the homeowner can cancel.
- **Risk: 501(c)(3) status is not actually in place.** No filing was located. Every argument about deductible employer gifts and grant eligibility depends on it.
  **Mitigant:** confirm status immediately. If it is pending, say "nonprofit in formation" publicly and do not solicit gifts as deductible until the determination letter arrives.
- **Risk: Article 6 sanitary triggers blow the budget on lots that looked feasible.**
  **Mitigant:** parcel-level sanitary pre-screen before any quote, sanitary priced inside the contract rather than as an allowance, and pursue a pre-reviewed standard detail with SCDHS.
- **Risk: the East End market is simply too small.** Realistic flow across all builders is 30 to 80 units per year, so the entire South Fork ADU market is $9 million to $24 million of annual revenue.
  **Mitigant:** underwrite the business plan on out-of-market expansion beginning year two and treat the East End as the reference portfolio, not the business.
- **Risk: political enthusiasm does not convert.** Warm board reception is not a permit, a site or a customer.
  **Mitigant:** convert access into something durable within ninety days, specifically a written demonstration site commitment and a pre-application conference with the Building Department and SCDHS. Enthusiasm for two local graduates has a short half-life.
- **Risk: positioning drift between workforce housing and extreme-environment deployable architecture** fragments a very small team across two incompatible businesses.
  **Mitigant:** pick one. The Sag Harbor workforce housing story is the one with political capital, subsidy access and a warm board behind it.

## 9. WHAT WOULD CHANGE THIS VIEW

The assessment above is built on public sources and the cost and regulatory work in the companion memo. Four pieces of private information would move it materially.

1. **Real factory quotes.** Budget pricing for a 600 sq ft all-electric DOS-insignia'd module from KBS, Westchester Modular and Simplex. If a module lands under $350 per sq ft delivered, the base case improves substantially and the homeowner payback drops toward eight years.
2. **A signed employer commitment.** One five-year master lease from a real Sag Harbor employer would validate the demand side and make the whole thing financeable. Its absence after a year of public profile is itself informative.
3. **Confirmed 501(c)(3) determination and any committed grant capital.** Runway determines whether they get to a second unit.
4. **Sag Harbor Village's ADU code revision.** The village is actively reconsidering the 20,000 sq ft and 70 ft thresholds that currently block six of eight applications. Relief would multiply the village-level opportunity and is the single largest swing factor in the local market.

## 10. BOTTOM LINE

The mission is right, the market is real, the political access is valuable and rare, and prefab is genuinely the correct delivery method for the highest-labor-cost residential market in the United States. Those are not small things and they are not easy to acquire.

The financial architecture, however, does not work. A revolving fund capitalized by a percentage of capped rent cannot compound, employers will not underwrite capex at $29,500 per bed per year when a stipend costs $12,000, the cost basis understates reality by roughly 2x, and the offer to homeowners is dominated by public programs already distributing $100.74 million on better terms.

**Restructured as a fee-for-service design-build practice that channels the public subsidy rather than competing with it, breakeven sits at roughly seven to eight units per year and the business clears $490,000 at fifteen units.** That is a real company. It is a small one, and getting it materially larger means leaving the East End.

The founders should be told the flywheel arithmetic plainly and early. It is the kind of error that is cheap to fix in September 2026 and extremely expensive to discover after a first raise has been made on the strength of it.
