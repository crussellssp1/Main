# WHO ACTUALLY BUILDS AN ADU, AND IS THE DEMAND REAL? (1 OF 1)

**Prepared:** September 23, 2026
**Subject:** Homeowner use cases with worked economics, and a test of whether Massachusetts ADU demand is already satisfied by existing rental stock
**Companion to:** `market-research.md`, `plinth-labs-assessment.md`, `northeast-market-comparison.md`

---

## 1. WHAT REALLY MATTERS

- **The land is free at the margin, and that is the entire economic trick.** A developer buying a lot in Newton to build a single 900 sq ft rental unit pays more for the dirt than the building and the deal dies. The homeowner already owns the dirt and has already paid for it. **ADUs are the only mechanism that adds small rental units to expensive single-family neighborhoods, because they are the only product where marginal land cost is zero.** That is why they exist as a category and why apartment construction does not substitute for them.
- **The largest driver is family, not yield.** A OnePoll survey puts multigenerational housing as the primary motivation for **61% of ADUs built**, with 73% of ADU owners citing accessibility and one in four housing or planning to house a disabled family member. AARP finds 67% of adults would consider living in an ADU to be near loved ones. The competing product is Massachusetts assisted living at a median of **$7,250 to $9,600 per month**, against which a $425,000 ADU pays back in **3.7 to 4.9 years.** Nothing else in this category has that payback.
- **The demand question is correct and the answer is not uniform.** Massachusetts rental vacancy rose from 3.40% in January 2025 to **4.90% in Q1 2026**. Greater Boston rent growth is **+1.04% year over year**. Colliers put larger managed buildings at 6.9% vacancy. Worcester rents **fell 0.68%.** The state added 34,500 homes in 2025 and is running ahead of pace on its 222,000-by-2035 goal. **The Massachusetts rental market outside the high-cost suburbs and the seasonal markets is no longer tight, and the investor case fails there on the arithmetic.**
- **Short-term rentals strengthen the demand case and weaken the revenue case simultaneously.** Airbnb removes year-round units from the stock (Provincetown has 27% of its housing registered as a short-term rental, the islands are 60% seasonal), which is why year-round scarcity persists in resort markets. But Massachusetts towns may prohibit short-term rental of a protected-use ADU, so **you cannot underwrite an ADU to nightly-rate yields.** It is a year-round rental product with year-round rental economics.
- **Correction to the prior memo.** I ranked sewered eastern Massachusetts gateway cities as a Tier 1 market. On rent data that is wrong for an investor yield play. Worcester at $2,156 for a two-bedroom against a $425,000 build is a **4.3% unlevered yield**, below the cost of debt. Those cities are high-volume ADU markets for **family-motivated** owners, not for investors.

## 2. THE SCREENING HEURISTIC

For a 900 sq ft ADU at roughly $425,000 all-in on a sewered lot:

| Target unlevered yield | Required NOI | Required gross rent | Monthly rent needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.0% | $21,250 | $30,357 | $2,530 |
| 6.5% | $27,625 | $39,464 | **$3,289** |
| 8.0% | $34,000 | $48,571 | $4,048 |

*Assumes 30% opex load covering property tax uplift, insurance, maintenance and vacancy.*

**Rule of thumb: an investor needs roughly $3,300 per month of achievable rent to make a 900 sq ft ADU work at a 6.5% unlevered yield.** Below that the deal depends on appreciation or on a non-rental motive.

## 3. FIVE WORKED EXAMPLES

### 3.1 The downsize-in-place arbitrage — the best pure economics

**Who:** An empty-nester couple in Medford or Newton, mid-60s, owning a 3-bedroom house outright or nearly so. They want to stay in the neighborhood. Selling means capital gains above the $500,000 exclusion, losing the property, and leaving the community.

**What Massachusetts uniquely permits:** 760 CMR 71.00 prohibits a municipality from requiring owner-occupancy of **either** the ADU or the principal dwelling. So they can build the ADU, **move into it themselves, and rent out the main house.** This is illegal under Southampton's ADU code, which requires the owner to occupy the principal dwelling year-round.

| Line | Value |
|---|---|
| 900 sq ft ADU, all-in, sewered lot | $425,000 |
| MassHousing detached ADU loan available | Up to $250,000 (primary residence, qualifies) |
| Rent on the vacated 3BR house, Boston metro average | $4,007/mo, $48,084/yr |
| Less opex at 30% | ($14,425) |
| **Net operating income** | **$33,659** |
| **Unlevered yield on cost** | **7.9%** |

They convert a dormant asset into $33,659 a year, cut their own operating and maintenance burden by moving into a new efficient unit, stay on their land, and release a family-sized home into a market that is short of them. This is the single most economically rational ADU in Massachusetts and almost nobody markets it this way.

### 3.2 Eldercare — the strongest payback in the category

**Who:** A couple in their 50s with an aging parent who can no longer live alone. 61% of ADUs nationally are built for exactly this.

| Line | Conservative | 2026 median |
|---|---|---|
| Massachusetts assisted living, monthly | $7,250 | $9,600 |
| Annual | $87,000 | $115,200 |
| 900 sq ft accessible ADU, all-in | $425,000 | $425,000 |
| **Years to break even versus assisted living** | **4.9** | **3.7** |

After the parent no longer needs it, the unit converts to a rental worth roughly $28,000 to $34,000 of NOI a year in a decent submarket, or becomes the owners' own downsizing unit per §3.1. The asset never goes to zero.

**This buyer is not price-shopping a contractor.** They are comparing against a $9,600 monthly bill and an outcome nobody wants. It is the least price-sensitive, most urgent demand in the entire category, and it is completely independent of the housing crisis, rent levels, and vacancy rates. **If the rental market softens further, this segment does not move.**

### 3.3 The investor play where it works — Somerville

**Who:** A small investor who buys a single-family, adds a 900 sq ft detached ADU, and operates two units. Legal in Massachusetts because owner-occupancy cannot be required. Illegal in Southampton.

| Line | Value |
|---|---|
| 900 sq ft ADU, all-in | $425,000 |
| 2BR market rent | $3,400/mo, $40,800/yr |
| Less opex at 30% | ($12,240) |
| **Net operating income** | **$28,560** |
| **Unlevered yield on incremental cost** | **6.7%** |
| MassHousing financing | **Not available. Requires primary residence** |

It works, but it is thin, and the financing gap matters more than the yield. **Every subsidized program in Massachusetts and on the East End requires owner occupancy of the property.** A pure investor pays conventional or DSCR rates on the whole stack, which is precisely why the 4.3% Worcester version is not a deal.

### 3.4 The investor play where it fails — Worcester

| Line | Value |

Negative leverage against any realistic debt cost, in a submarket where rents are falling and new multifamily is actually delivering. **This is the deal that proves the user's instinct right: in much of Massachusetts, the rental demand genuinely is already being met.**

### 3.5 The seasonal market — Nantucket

| Line | Value |
|---|---|
| 900 sq ft ADU, all-in (island premium, barge, Title 5 nitrogen) | ~$525,000 (estimate, unverified) |

## 4. IS THE DEMAND ALREADY MET?

Three separate questions hide inside this, and they have different answers.

### 4.1 Does existing rental stock substitute? Mostly no, for a structural reason

The Massachusetts housing deficit is 222,000 units from 2025 to 2035. The state added 34,500 in 2025, ahead of pace. But production is concentrated in multifamily on land where multifamily pencils, which is not single-family suburbs. **There is no economic mechanism other than the ADU that puts a small rental unit on a single-family lot in Newton, Milton or Chatham,** because a developer would have to buy the land and the land is the cost. The homeowner's land is already sunk.

So ADUs do not compete with apartment supply. They serve a different geography and a different unit size, and the two are not fungible to a tenant who needs to be in a specific town for a school, a job or a parent.

### 4.2 Do Airbnbs substitute? No, they are the cause

Short-term rentals **remove** year-round units. Provincetown has 27% of its housing registered as a short-term rental with the state. Nantucket and Martha's Vineyard are 60% seasonal. A 2016 UMass study found that a one standard deviation increase in Airbnb listings within a census tract associates with a 0.4% rise in asking rents in Boston, and Wachsmuth at McGill attributed a 1.4% rent increase in Boston between 2014 and 2017 to Airbnb alone.

**The catch cuts the other way on revenue.** Massachusetts towns may restrict or prohibit short-term rental of a protected-use ADU, and the statute's protection against unreasonable restriction explicitly does not extend to short-term use. So the ADU cannot chase nightly rates. It is a year-round product competing at year-round rents. **Airbnb is a reason the demand exists and a use you cannot capture.**

### 4.3 Is the rental market tight enough to support the yield? Increasingly, no, outside specific submarkets

This is where the instinct to push back is correct.

| Indicator | Reading |
|---|---|
| Massachusetts rental vacancy, Jan 2025 | 3.40% |
| Massachusetts rental vacancy, Q1 2026 | **4.90%** |
| Greater Boston rent growth, YoY | **+1.04%** |
| Boston larger managed buildings vacancy, Q1 2026 (Colliers) | 6.9% |
| Greater Boston real-time vacancy, projected Sept 2026 | **north of 3%, highest since the pandemic** |
| Worcester rent, YoY | **-0.68%** |

Commentary describes the market as having "climbed out of the historic supply shortage of 2022" with "pricing softness." **Any ADU business underwriting to 2022-era rent growth in the Boston metro is underwriting to a market that no longer exists.**

Where the scarcity is genuinely structural and unresolved: the **seasonal markets** (Cape, islands, Provincetown, and by extension the East End), where short-term rental conversion permanently removes year-round stock and no amount of apartment construction elsewhere helps; and the **high-cost inner suburbs**, where nothing small gets built because land cost forbids it.

## 5. RANKING THE CUSTOMER SEGMENTS

| Rank | Segment | Share of market | Payback | Price sensitivity | Sensitive to rent softening |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Eldercare and multigenerational** | ~61% | 3.7 to 4.9 yrs vs assisted living | Lowest | **No** |
| 2 | **Downsize-in-place** | Not measured; enabled by MA rules | 12.6 yrs, 7.9% yield | Low | Moderate |
| 3 | **Investor yield** | Small, geographically narrow | 6.7% at best, needs $3,300+/mo rent | Highest | **Yes, severely** |
| 4 | **Employer / workforce** | Smallest | Requires employer to act against own economics | n/a | No, but demand is political not commercial |

**On the investor angle specifically:** the instinct is right that it beats workforce housing, and right that Massachusetts is the place it becomes legal at all, because owner-occupancy cannot be required. But it is third, not first, for three reasons. It works in a handful of submarkets rather than statewide. It is the only segment locked out of every subsidized financing program, all of which require primary residence. And it is the only segment whose demand evaporates if rents keep softening, which they currently are.

**Eldercare is the segment to lead with.** It is the majority of actual demand, it has the best payback of anything in the category by a wide margin, it is defended against a soft rental market, and the buyer is comparing against $9,600 a month rather than against a competing contractor's bid.

## 6. WHAT THIS MEANS FOR A PRODUCT

- **Design for accessibility as the default, not the option.** Single level, zero-threshold entry, 36-inch doors, curbless shower, blocking in the bathroom walls for future grab bars, lever hardware. It costs very little at the factory and it serves 61% of the market directly, 73% of owners who cite accessibility, and every eventual resale. A unit that cannot take a wheelchair is unsellable to the largest segment.
- **Build at the full 900 sq ft where the principal dwelling allows it.** Two bedrooms serves eldercare with a live-in aide, serves a downsizing couple, and clears the rent threshold the investor case requires. A studio serves none of them well.
- **Market the downsize-in-place case explicitly.** It is the best economics available, it is newly legal in Massachusetts, and no competitor is framing it.
- **Do not underwrite a pipeline on gateway-city investor demand.** Worcester, Lowell and Lawrence will generate permit volume from family-motivated owners. They will not generate investor deals at $425,000 of cost.
- **Screen markets on achievable rent of $3,300 per month before anything else,** then on sewer, then on entitlement.
